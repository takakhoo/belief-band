# Asset-class trading costs for the no-trade-band section

Compiled 2026-10-08. Every number below was read from the primary document unless the "Status" column says otherwise. BibTeX for verified sources is in `refs_costs.bib`. "One-way" means the cost of a single buy or sell relative to the efficient (mid) price; "round-trip" means a buy plus a later sell. A quoted bid-ask spread is a round-trip number for a trade that crosses it twice; the one-way cost is half of it.

Status codes: **V** = read in the primary source. **V2** = read in a reputable secondary source that cites the primary (survey article, trade press). **D** = derived by me from verified inputs (arithmetic shown). **UNVERIFIED** = could not confirm; do not cite as-is.

## Notes

1. **E-mini spread.** Fett & Haynes say the E-mini spread stayed "very close to the minimum tick size" over 2013 to mid-2016, but they quote the tick as "$25" per contract. The CME E-mini S&P 500 tick is 0.25 index points × $50 = $12.50. I could not reconcile this; either they normalize differently or it is a typo. Cite the qualitative finding (spread pinned at one tick) and compute bps from the contract tick yourself (row 2). CME's own "Big Picture" futures-vs-ETF cost study (1.25 bps per tick for ES vs. 0.5 bps for SPY, $3.54 per contract commission, 2016) could not be fetched: cmegroup.com returned an anti-scraping block. **UNVERIFIED.**
2. **Equity costs are one-way.** FIM report per-trade price impact relative to the pre-trade price. Double them for a round trip. Their large-cap value-weighted mean (12.6-14.7 bps one-way) is the right institutional number for block-sized rebalancing; the index-fund replication cost (4.7 bps per year) is the right number for a passive sleeve.
3. **Corporate bond one-way vs. round-trip.** Bessembinder et al. use the indicator-variable regression ΔP = c·ΔQ with Q = ±1. The 2017 BJMV draft calls c the "round trip" cost (0.42% for 2014-2016). BSV (2020), written by two of the same authors, label the same series "one-way" (Fig. 3 caption) and report 0.84% round-trip, exactly 2× the draft numbers by trade-size bucket. Under BSV's convention the IG and HY figures in rows 18-19 are one-way, so round-trip is roughly 0.76% (IG) and 1.02% (HY) for 2014-2016. Cite BSV (2020) for the round-trip level, and check the published JF Table III (paywalled for me) before quoting the IG/HY breakdown with a JF page number.
4. **Treasury spreads are interdealer.** SR 827 and BSV report interdealer (BrokerTec/GovPX) quoted spreads. Dealer-to-customer costs for institutions are higher and are not public; I found no verified customer-level number. The quoted spread is round-trip; one-way for the 10-year is about 0.9 bps.
5. **PE secondary prices measure a discount to stale NAV.** Jefferies prices are averages over the LP portfolio deals they see, so they measure a price discount to NAV rather than a microstructure transaction cost. Two biases point in opposite directions: NAVs lag public markets (so the 2022 discount partly reflects stale marks), and deferred-payment structures inflate headline prices (~400 bps on deals that use them in 2024, row 27). NSVW (p. 10) make the related point that a discount on NAV understates or overstates the economic cost depending on unfunded commitments. For a no-trade band, treat (1 − price/NAV) as an upper bound on the one-way cost of exiting.
6. **Denominator effect.** I found no IMF, BIS, Fed, ECB or BoE publication that measures the denominator effect with numbers (searched GFSR, FSR, FSR-style sites and BIS; several central-bank and IMF PDFs blocked automated download, so a mention could exist). The prior conclusion in `literature.md` stands: there is no peer-reviewed empirical paper on it. The best citable evidence: BCG/IESE (2009) for the 2008-09 episode with concrete target changes; Anzia & Spindel (2024) for FY2022 median actual PE share (~12%) above the median target (10%). Caveat on Anzia & Spindel: the two medians come from the same 206-plan PPD sample but the authors do not compute plan-level overweights, and they never use the phrase "denominator effect". NSVW (2019) supply the 2009 secondary-market price collapse and seller composition.
7. **Version caveats.** NSVW and Kargar et al. numbers come from NBER working papers; BJMV from the May 2017 draft. The published versions may differ. FIM "Trading Costs" remains an SSRN working paper (Crossref lists no journal).
